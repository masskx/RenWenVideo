A Note On Lattice Definition of Gauge Theories With
Chiral Fermions

Edward Witten
School of Natural Sciences, Institute for Advanced Study,
1 Einstein Drive, Princeton, NJ 08540 USA

Abstract: Because of the fermion doubling problem, it has been a longstanding challenge
to describe a lattice algorithm for studying four-dimensional gauge theories coupled to
chiral fermions. Recently, an algorithm to do so has been described by Craig, extending
partial results in the previous literature. Here we describe a variant of this algorithm,
based on interpolating a lattice gauge field over the continuum (as in Craig’s work) and
coupling the interpolated gauge field to continuum chiral fermions. The same variant was
also proposed recently by Hsu. In any dimension and for any anomaly-free representation,
this approach can be implemented on a computer in principle, but not in practice by Monte
Carlo methods because the chiral fermion path integral is not positive.

Introduction

Lattice gauge theory [1] provides a very satisfactory regularization of four-dimensional
Yang-Mills theory, and has offered an effective starting point for nonperturbative numerical exploration of this theory, as pioneered in [2]. The same framework is applicable – with
considerably more difficulty – for gauge theories like QCD with fermions in vector-like
representations. However, because of the fermion doubling problem, which is well characterized by the Nielsen-Ninomiya theorem [3], it has been notoriously difficult to describe in
this framework gauge theories with chiral fermions, such as the Standard Model of particle
physics.
Ginsparg and Wilson proposed a strategy to circumvent the fermion doubling problem
[4]. Building on the domain wall fermions of Kaplan [5], Narayanan and Neuberger [6, 7]
interpreted the chiral fermion determinant as an overlap. Neuberger [8] and Hasenfratz
[9] then derived lattice Dirac operators that satisfy the Ginsparg-Wilson relation, and
Lüscher [10] showed that this implies an exact chiral symmetry of the classical lattice action.
But this framework does not provide any immediate way to define a suitable measure for
integration over these fermions, so quantization is not obvious. However, Lüscher was
able to define such a measure for chiral gauge theories with abelian gauge group [11],
thus achieving a lattice regularization for such theories. One important ingredient in this
construction was a restriction to “admissible” gauge fields – gauge fields whose plaquette
curvatures are small.
The extension of Lüscher’s construction to nonabelian gauge theory was not straightforward. Lüscher himself showed that a nonabelian version of his construction works to
all orders of perturbation theory around the trivial connection [12], and there were several

–1–

other partial results [13–15]. Very recently, Craig overcame the difficulties and, with the
aid of several new ideas, finally constructed a lattice regularization of chiral gauge theories
in enough generality to encompass the Standard Model [16].
In this article, we will revisit this subject, and explain a slightly different route to the
objective using two key facts. The second of these facts was also used by Craig, and the
first is closely related to facts that he used. Actually, following the appearance of Craig’s
paper and while the present one was being written, another paper appeared by Hsu [17]
also describing a lattice regularization of chiral fermions and using the same key facts that
the present article is based on. We will say more about Hsu’s paper and the background
to it in section 1.2.
Our analysis gives several generalizations of previous results (very likely the arguments
in [16, 17] can be modestly extended to yield these generalizations). First, we use no special
properties of the gauge group or fermion representation, so in four dimensions our analysis
applies for any gauge group and anomaly-free fermion representation. Second (provided
one follows the general analysis in section 2.4 of [18] or in the appendix of [19] rather than
the simpler explanation we give in section 2.1), our discussion generalizes to any anomalyfree fermion system in any dimension, such as chiral fermions in two dimensions or more
exotic cases such as nonperturbative anomalies of three-dimensional fermions in theories
with time-reversal symmetry. Finally, our discussion applies on an arbitrary compact
Euclidean spin manifold, not necessarily a torus. One might question the importance of
this generalization, as lattice gauge theory is usually formulated on a torus. However,
since gravity does exist in the real world, part of the consistency of anomaly-free chiral
gauge theories such as the Standard Model is that they can be consistently coupled to
gravity. And part of the consistency of that coupling is that these models can be defined
in Euclidean signature on any spin manifold.
We now summarize the two key facts that we will use. This summary is detailed
enough to explain the main ideas of the paper.
1.1

The Fermion Path Integral In A Background Field

The first key fact is that in continuum Yang-Mills theory with any gauge group G, the path
integral for fermions in any anomaly-free representation, chiral or not, coupled to any classical background field A (and any background spacetime metric), is always well-defined. The
representation has to be free of both perturbative and nonperturbative (global) anomalies.
This statement may sound like a tautology; if the fermion path integral in some representation is not well-defined, we declare that this representation is anomalous. What gives
the statement its force is the following:
(1) In learning how to define the fermion path integral for anomaly-free theories, one
also learns to determine all possible nonperturbative anomalies – that is, anomalies beyond
the standard perturbative anomalies. For example, in four-dimensional chiral gauge theories with connected and simply-connected gauge group, one learns [18, 20] that the only

As written, the formula (1.1) appears to depend on the choice of the five-manifold Y
and the extension of the gauge field A over Y . However, it turns out that the fermion representation R is anomaly-free if and only if the exponentiated eta-invariant exp(−iπη(DY ))
depends only on W and the gauge field, metric, and spin structure of W , not on Y or the

a technical explanation). First of all, perturbative anomalies in d dimensions are related
to Chern-Simons forms in d + 1 dimensions [24, 25]. This relation is beautifully expressed
in the idea of anomaly inflow from an extra dimension [26]. If and only if a representation
R has no perturbative anomaly, the Chern-Simons form that would be related to that
representation by anomaly inflow vanishes. The eta-invariant of a connection is locally
equivalent to the integral of a Chern-Simons form, but globally it captures some additional
information. It turns out that to extend the relation between anomalies and Chern-Simons
to global or nonperturbative anomalies, one just has to use the eta-invariant instead of
Chern-Simons. This was first shown in [27], with an important later generalization by Dai
and Freed [28]. The analysis in [27] was based on computing the holonomy around a loop in
field space of what is nowadays often called the Berry connection, or in this context sometimes the Bismut-Freed connection [29], of the fermion ground state. It was shown that
this holonomy can be expressed in terms of the eta-invariant. See also [30] for a closely
related analysis. The upshot is the following: a fermion representation R is completely
anomaly-free if and only if, for any manifold Y without boundary, exp(−iπη(DY )) = 1.
When this fact is combined with the gluing law for the eta-invariant that was proved in
[28], one learns that the right hand side of eqn. (1.1) is independent of the choice of Y ,
and of the extension of A over Y , if and only if the representation R is anomaly-free. The
formula (1.1) was most fully justified from a physical point of view in [18]. (The Dai-Freed
theorem – as opposed to the formula (1.1) that it suggests – was elucidated from a physical
point of view in [31].) The formula (1.1) can be viewed as an extension of the fact that
anomaly inflow for domain wall fermions can be described via the eta-invariant [32]. This
formula has had a variety of applications, for example to the worldsheet path integral of
the heterotic string [33].

1.2

Interpolating A Lattice Gauge Field To The Continuum

Now we come to the second key fact. It may not be obvious that understanding the path
integral for anomaly-free chiral fermions in the continuum is going to help us construct a
nonperturbative regularization of chiral gauge theories. For that purpose, we presumably
need to consider lattice gauge fields. What good will it do to know what happens if chiral
fermions are coupled to continuum gauge fields? The answer to this question is clear from
the construction described by Craig [16] and the further discussion by Hsu [17].
By way of background, long ago Lüscher [36] introduced the idea of constraining lattice
gauge fields so that every plaquette holonomy UP is close to the identity, say |UP − 1| < ϵ
for some fixed and sufficiently small ϵ. Lattice gauge fields with this property have been
called “admissible.” The original goal was to define topological invariants such as instanton
number in lattice gauge theory. In conventional lattice gauge theory, with the link variables
being general gauge group elements as in [1], it is not possible to define a topological
invariant such as the instanton number, because an instanton can continuously shrink to
a point and disappear in the holes between the lattice sites. However, if one constrains
the lattice gauge field so that all plaquette holonomies are sufficiently close to the identity,
then the instanton number (and corresponding topological invariants in other dimensions)
can indeed be defined.
It was further argued [36] that restricting to admissible lattice gauge fields does not
affect the continuum limit of the theory. The reason for this expectation is that if g is the
gauge coupling at the lattice scale, then typically |UP −1| ∼ g, which because of asymptotic
freedom becomes much less than ϵ near the continuum limit. The admissibility constraint
is hence expected to become irrelevant in the continuum limit.
Another interpretation of the fact that instanton number can be defined for admissible
gauge fields is that admissible lattice gauge fields can be interpolated over the continuum,
as shown in various degrees of generality in [37–40] and [16]. In other words, given an
admissible lattice gauge field, one can interpolate it to a corresponding continuum gauge
field that agrees with the lattice gauge field on the lattice links. To make such an interpolation involves arbitrary choices, but the choices can be made in a way that respects all
lattice symmetries and is gauge-invariant (if two lattice gauge fields are gauge-equivalent,
then the corresponding interpolations are also gauge-equivalent) and in such a way that
the continuum gauge field associated to a given admissible lattice gauge field always has
field strength (in lattice units) at most of order ϵ. Under this condition, the topology of
the continuum gauge field does not depend on the choices. We will refer to such a procedure as continuum interpolation of admissible lattice gauge fields. Once one knows that
an admissible lattice gauge field has a continuum interpolation, one can simply define its
topological invariants to be those of the interpolation.

Continuum interpolations exist for every compact gauge group G and on any manifold
of any dimension d. We give one explanation of this in section 3; this can also be deduced
by a slight extension of arguments of ’t Hooft [39] and Craig [16], among others.
Craig makes use of the existence of continuum interpolations to define a regularization
of chiral gauge theories as follows. As explained earlier, overlap fermions and the GinspargWilson equation give a way to define chiral fermions on a lattice, but without a simple
way to define a measure for integrating over them. To determine such a measure, Craig
compares chiral fermions on ever finer lattices, ultimately defining the fermion measure
via a continuum limit. The continuum interpolation of the original lattice gauge fields is
used to define a gauge field that can be coupled to the lattice fermions on any scale. In
short, Craig uses the behavior near a continuum limit to define a measure for lattice chiral
fermions. In part, this construction involves new ab initio proofs of some of the facts that
were cited in section 1.1 to explain the well-definedness of the chiral fermion path integral.
Alternatively, and as described independently of the present work by Hsu [17], once
one knows that lattice gauge theory can be restricted to admissible lattice gauge fields and
that admissible lattice gauge fields have a continuum interpolation, one can construct a
lattice regularization of chiral gauge theories by directly using the fact that the continuum
path integral of anomaly-free chiral fermions is always well-defined. In fact, there have
been a number of earlier proposals along roughly these lines, including [41–46] and [39].
The main novelty of the present article is possibly to incorporate a more precise description
of the chiral fermion path integral and its anomalies.
The approach we discuss applies in any spacetime dimension, for any anomaly-free
fermion representation; the only lattice specific fact that is used is the existence of continuum interpolations. One simply couples admissible lattice gauge fields, or more precisely
their continuum interpolation, to continuum chiral fermions. There is no fermion doubling
problem because one never introduces lattice fermions (as explained in section 4, numerical
evaluation does not bring back the doubling problem). The chiral fermion path integral is
then characterized by the formula (1.1) (or its generalization, explained in section 2.3, in
instanton sectors). Thus the lattice path integral is defined by an integral over admissible
families of link variables U⟨xy⟩ connecting neighboring lattice sites x, y, weighted by the

Organization of This Article

In section 2 of this article, we will provide more detail on the well-definedness of the path
integral for anomaly-free chiral fermions coupled to a background gauge field. In section
3, we explain one approach to constructing continuum interpolations of admissible lattice
gauge fields, for an arbitrary compact gauge group G and on a manifold of any dimension.
Finally, in section 4, we address the following question: Could the regularization of chiral
gauge theories described here be implemented on a computer in principle, even if not very
efficiently? One may worry that when one tries to actually evaluate numerically the formula
for the chiral fermion path integral that we describe, the fermion doubling problem would
come back. We will see that that is not the case.

2.3

Theta-Angles And Fermion Zero-Modes

There are two deficiencies in the explanation that we have given so far. First, we assumed
that the four-manifold W on which we wish to study a given chiral gauge theory is the
boundary of a five-manifold Y over which the spin structure and gauge field of W extend.
In other words, we assumed that W is trivial as an element of the relevant cobordism group.

– 16 –

Second, we assumed that the partition function of the chiral fermions on W is generically
nonzero.
The importance of this second assumption is that if the partition function is generically
nonzero, then the path integral of fermions coupled to background gauge and gravitational
fields is adequately described by giving the formula (2.18) for that partition function. The
only additional information that one needs to compute a path integral with insertions
of operators containing fermions is a knowledge of the fermion propagator, and in the
absence of zero-modes, this is always well-defined for any representation, anomaly-free or
not. However, if the fermion partition function vanishes identically, then to characterize the
fermion path integral, we need to replace eqn. (2.18) by a similar formula for a correlation
function that is generically nonzero. Such a formula will be described at the end of this
section.
The two obstructions are closed related, since when the fermion partition function
vanishes identically due to an index theorem (or, in some spacetime dimensions, a mod
2 index theorem), this always represents an obstruction to null-cobordism of W , that is,
to the existence of Y . However, the cobordism obstruction is more general since it is
possible to have obstructions in the cobordism problem that are not reflected in fermion
zero-modes. For example, in four dimensions, this can happen in chiral gauge theories with
suitable gauge groups. Therefore, we will consider first the cobordism problem, ignoring the
second question about fermion zero-modes, and then explain how to modify the definitions
to take into account the fact that the fermion path integral vanishes identically in some
topological sectors.

result is a smooth continuum interpolation procedure that is gauge-invariant, preserves all
lattice symmetries, reproduces the link variables exactly, and on each cell depends only on
the lattice data of that cell.

Can This Regularization Be Implemented on a Computer?

Finally, we will address this question: can the regularization of chiral gauge theories that
we have discussed, with lattice gauge fields interpolated over the continuum and coupled
to continuum chiral fermions, be implemented on a computer?
There will certainly be no straightforward lattice Monte Carlo implementation, because the chiral fermion path integral, even when anomaly-free, lacks the positivity needed
in Monte Carlo procedures. So an implementation, at least with known methods, may
be impracticably slow. But is such an implementation possible in principle? In lattice
gauge theory with a finite volume and a positive lattice spacing, and therefore only finitely
many link variables, the lattice path integral is an integral over a compact space of finite
dimension. So if the chiral fermion integral can be effectively computed, then the whole
lattice path integral can be effectively computed as well, in principle.
So we have to discuss whether one can effectively interpolate an admissible lattice
gauge field over the continuum, extend it over some Y , and then compute
